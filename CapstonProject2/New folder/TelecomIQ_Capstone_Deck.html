The honesty check that changed the project

We deleted our own best score — on purpose

Two columns in the subscriber file fail the day-before test: would this value exist, with this value, the day before the subscriber churned? Both had to come out — and taking them out cost us a fifth of our headline number.

0.217

of ROC-AUC was pure leakage. A model scoring 0.946 would have been presented as a success, deployed, and predicted nothing — because on the day you need a score, nobody has sent the retention offer yet.

ROC-AUC, identical splits, one difference

Same features, same forest, same seed — only the two leaking columns move.

removed retention_offer_sent

Sent to 84.3% of leavers and 5.1% of stayers. The offer was triggered by a churn signal somebody had already spotted. Feeding it back in asks the model to predict the past.

removed total_charges

Correlates 0.9995 with ARPU × tenure. Perfectly valid for revenue reporting; wrong for this target, because it contains the answer.

Leakage is a property of the pairing of feature and target, not of a column on its own — which is why the audit is re-run whenever the target changes. 0.729 is our honest number, and every rupee figure in this deck is built on it.

The uncomfortable part — say it out loud

At 19.6 to one the arithmetic wants us to ring 742 of 800 subscribers. That is a real answer, and it tells us something true: at these costs the binding constraint is not the model, it is how many calls the retention team can actually make. So we set the threshold against capacity as well as cost — and on slide 12 we show why the cheapest threshold here is not the one we ship.

The central finding

The riskiest segment complains — it doesn't overspend

Four behavioural segments, clustered on usage and tenure only — region and plan were kept out of the clustering and used afterwards to describe what we found.

Churn rate against spend — bubble area is segment size

One segment sits far above the book average, and it is not the expensive one.

Segment 0 — 392 subscribers, 12.2% of the base

Churns at 40% against a book average of 24.0% — 1.7× the risk. Its defining feature is 3.37 complaints in six months against 0.46–0.69 everywhere else. Mid-ARPU, mid-tenure, light data use. These are not price-sensitive customers. They are annoyed customers.

But the biggest bill is not the biggest rate

Segment 1 is half the book and churns at only 23% — yet it carries ₹22.0L of lifetime value at risk, more than twice segment 0's ₹9.2L. Rate tells you who to worry about; rate × size × value tells you where the money is.

We chose k = 4 on a commercial test, not a statistical one: inertia fell smoothly with no elbow and silhouette drifted down from 0.238 (k=2) to 0.158 (k=8), so the data offered no natural answer. Four is the number of genuinely different campaigns the retention team could run.

What actually happens when the model says 0.62

A score nobody acts on is not a finding. Here is the physical path from the nightly job to a retention agent's dialler — and the one line of arithmetic that decides who is on it.

Why we rank on value and not on risk

A list sorted by risk alone tells the retention team to spend the same ₹300 on a ₹120-a-month subscriber as on an ₹800 one. Both calls cost the same; the two outcomes are worth 6.7× different amounts. Ranking on expected loss reorders the list substantially — the top-300 by risk and the top-300 by expected loss are not the same 300 people, and the difference is where the return comes from.

The trap we avoided

The scoring model is refit on all 3,200 rows so that everyone gets a score. We report no metric from that refit — every number in this deck comes from the held-out fold. Scoring and evaluating are two different jobs, and mixing them is how an honest team accidentally publishes a fabricated number.

A second finding, free from the same data

192 subscriber-months nobody had flagged

An isolation forest over monthly behaviour surfaces three patterns hiding in the usage file: SIM-box bypass, subscription fraud, and revenue leakage — genuine usage billed at exactly zero. None of them were labelled.

Sensitivity is a staffing decision in disguise

The revenue-assurance team is two people and can genuinely investigate about 8 cases a week.

To justify it we would need either a third analyst or evidence from the first hundred investigations that the hit rate is high enough to pay for one — which is exactly what this quarter's queue produces.

A flag is a case, never a conclusion

Contamination is not discovered in the data — it is a decision about how many cases you are willing to open, dressed up as a parameter. We hand-classified the top twenty flags with subscriber context attached; several were ordinary heavy users and not a problem at all.

Break-even, which is the number to remember

The call only pays if it prevents more than 12.8% of the churn in the group. The credit needs 23.4%. Our assumed 15% clears the first bar by 2.2 points — a margin far too thin to spend a lakh on with confidence.

The stress test

Halve the assumption everything rests on

If a retention call is half as effective as we were told — 7.5% instead of 15% — both segment-wide campaigns lose money. A recommendation that does not survive this test is worth knowing about before we stand up, not after.

Net value at the assumed effectiveness, and at half of it

Blanketing a segment does not survive

Ringing all 392 subscribers in segment 0 turns a ₹20,698 gain into a ₹48,451 loss. The bill credit turns into a ₹1,00,352 loss. The reason is simple: 60% of the people we would ring were never going to leave, and we pay ₹300 for every one of them.

survives Targeting on model score does

At a model score of 0.50 or above, 74% of the people we ring are genuine churners rather than 40%. That is 92 subscribers, ₹27,600 of spend, and +₹32,376 net — more than either segment-wide campaign, on a quarter of the budget, and still positive when we halve the assumption.

The finding hiding in this slide

Slide 6's cost-optimal threshold was 0.10 — but that arithmetic assumed catching a churner saves them. Once the intervention only works 15% of the time, 0.10 loses ₹74 per contact and the optimum flips to the opposite end of the range. The cost of a false negative is not the lifetime value; it is the lifetime value times how often you can actually do something about it.

Why not just ring everybody?

Because the maths that says "ring 742 of 800" prices a missed churner at the full ₹5,880 — as if catching them guaranteed keeping them. It does not. Pricing the intervention honestly shrinks the list by 88% and makes it the only version that pays.

Honest limitation, and the next week

The number our recommendation depends on most is the one we do not have

The limitation

Nothing in this dataset says a retention call works. Not once.

Every rupee figure in this deck runs through one number we were handed rather than measured: that a call prevents 15% of churn. Our data contains no campaign, no control group and no outcome — so the sign of our recommendation is an assumption, not a finding. Break-even is 12.8%; we assumed 15%. If the true figure is 11%, the right answer is to do nothing and we would not know it from anything in here.

We chose the narrow, high-precision list because it is the version that still pays at 7.5% — not because we believe 15%. That is robustness standing in for evidence, and it is not the same thing.

Also on the record

157 of 3,200 subscribers carry median-filled values (flagged, never hidden). Our shipped threshold knowingly misses most churners — recall is 0.089, and we accept that because the calls we do make are the ones that pay. Six months of history is thin for a churn model; segment boundaries will move.

What we are not claiming

That segment 0's complaints cause its churn. The association is strong (3.37 vs 0.5) and the direction is plausible, but this is observational data and we did not run an experiment. "Investigate the complaints" is a hypothesis worth a quarter of somebody's time, not a proven mechanism.

What is true

We lose 24% of 3,200 subscribers — ₹45.2 lakh of lifetime value — and our only current warning system is a retention offer sent by hand after somebody notices. An honest model scores 0.729, not the 0.946 we could have claimed.
